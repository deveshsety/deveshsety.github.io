Predicting Marketing-Campaign Response with a Decision Tree (CART)¶

Business question: Given customer attributes, who is most likely to respond to a marketing campaign — and how much of the campaign budget can be saved by contacting only the best-scoring customers?

Structure adapted from the course CART template (Great Lakes, Business Analytics). Extended with ROC-AUC-based hyperparameter tuning, a lift / cumulative-gains analysis, customer-segment profiling and a targeting recommendation.

2. Prepare the data¶

  • Cust_ID is a unique identifier (one value per row), so it carries no predictive signal. It is dropped.
  • AGE_BKT is a bucketed copy of Age, so it is dropped to avoid duplicating information.
  • Gender and Occupation are nominal categories with several levels, so they are one-hot encoded rather than given arbitrary numeric ranks.
  • The data is split 70:30 into train and test sets. All model tuning uses the training set only; the test set is used once, for evaluation.

3. Baseline: why accuracy alone is misleading¶

Only about 9% of customers respond. A "model" that predicts no response for everyone would therefore already look very accurate. Any tree has to be judged against this baseline, and with ranking metrics (AUC, lift) that reflect how the model would actually be used to pick customers.

Baseline test accuracy (predict 'no response' for everyone): 90.9%

4. Three trees, compared¶

  1. Unpruned tree (the course's starting model): grows until it memorises noise.
  2. Hand-pruned tree: depth, split and leaf-size limits chosen by inspecting the tree.
  3. Tuned tree: GridSearchCV with 5-fold cross-validation on the training set, scored on ROC-AUC. AUC is used instead of accuracy because with a 9% response rate, accuracy rewards models that simply predict "no".

Reading the table

  • The unpruned tree fits the training data best but generalises worst (largest train-test gap).
  • The hand-pruned tree fixes most of the accuracy gap, but at depth 13 it still has over 100 leaves and ranks customers worse on unseen data (lower test AUC).
  • The CV-tuned tree is much simpler (26 leaves versus 126 and 410), overfits far less (small AUC gap) and has the highest test AUC. It is used as the final model.
  • Test accuracy is almost identical for all three and close to the baseline, which is exactly why accuracy is the wrong yardstick here.

5. Evaluating the final model¶

Confusion matrix at the default 0.5 cutoff. A leaf only predicts "respond" when more than half of its customers responded. With a ~9% base rate, no leaf of the final tree reaches that level, so at the default cutoff the model never flags anyone as a responder (precision and recall are both 0). That does not make the model useless: its predicted probabilities still rank customers well, which is how it would be used in practice (Sections 6 and 7). 6. Lift and cumulative gains: how well does the model rank customers?¶

What this does. Instead of asking the tree for a yes/no answer, we take each test customer's probability of responding, sort customers from highest to lowest, and cut them into 10 equal groups (deciles). Then we check what really happened in each group, using the test-set outcomes the model never saw.

  • response_rate: actual share of responders in the decile.
  • lift: response_rate / overall response rate. A lift of 3 means that group responds 3x as often as an average customer; 1 means no better than random.
  • cum_resp: share of all responders captured if you contact deciles 1 to N.
  • cum_lift: cumulative capture divided by the share of customers contacted.

Recommendation¶

  • Score every customer and contact in priority order rather than mass-mailing the whole base. On the held-out test set, the top 30% of customers by score contained ~64% of all responders, so mailing 70% fewer customers would still reach roughly two thirds of the people who respond.
  • Best-fit profile: short holding period, high SCR score, many credit transactions and self-employed occupation (Section 7).
  • Choose the cut-off with costs. The right share of customers to contact depends on the cost per contact and the value of a response. The scenario table shows the trade-off at each level, and a cost-based cutoff is the natural next step.

Limitations and next steps¶

  • Single 70:30 split. Figures come from one held-out set of 6,000 customers (545 responders); repeated cross-validation would give confidence intervals.
  • Modest ranking power. Test AUC is ~0.77. That is useful but far from perfect. At the default 0.5 cutoff the model flags no one, so it should be used as a score with a deliberately chosen cutoff, not as a yes/no classifier.
  • Tied scores. A small tree produces a limited set of distinct probabilities, so deciles are coarse (see the tie-robust check in Section 6).
  • Correlation, not causation. The tree describes who responded in the past, not what would make a new customer respond.
  • Next steps: compare with a random forest or gradient boosting, tune the decision cutoff on campaign cost/benefit, and validate on a later campaign.
